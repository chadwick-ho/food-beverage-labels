Die-Cut Liner Breaks: Map the Failure to the Label Repeat

Neat complaint scraps remove the relationship between a liner break and the label repeat, making competing explanations difficult to compare.

Updated: 22 October 2026 | English B2B packaging buyer guide.

Editorial purchasing guidance. Packaging examples are illustrative.

Several liner breaks look similar, but the samples arrive trimmed into small scraps. The converter can no longer tell whether each break began at the same label corner, the same web edge or a different position.

Keep a repeat-position map with the complete adjacent labels before asking for a tooling conclusion. Repeated geometry is evidence to investigate, not automatic proof of the cause.

Give the sample a direction and an origin

Keep the two sides of the break together and enough neighboring repeat to identify the label shape. Mark the direction of travel on separate identification material without covering the feature. Record which liner face is photographed and how the sample was obtained under the site's safe equipment procedure.

Use a simple diagram of the actual label repeat to identify the observed start location. A description such as bottom left is ambiguous when one person views the printed side and another views the liner from behind. Name the viewing side and leading edge before using corner names.

The map is an observation aid. It does not assign a die-pressure limit or require the buyer to perform an improvised destructive test.

Separate the start from the path of the tear

A tear that eventually crosses a label corner did not necessarily start there. Preserve both edges of the failed piece and photograph its context. If the start cannot be identified, write unknown and ask the converter to inspect the retained material rather than marking the most visually dramatic point as the origin.

For a hypothetical series of breaks, compare sample maps before changing the stock. Consistent position relative to the repeat supports a different next investigation from inconsistent positions at a damaged web edge. Neither pattern alone rules out machine-path conditions or more than one contributor.

Ask for the converter's corresponding production view

Request the available lane and run references for the delivered rolls. The supplier may be able to compare retained material across the source web or different parts of the run. If the conversion history cannot connect the returned strip to a lane, keep that gap visible; do not manufacture a lane number from the finished roll width.

Agree what additional inspection or representative dispensing evaluation is needed with the responsible suppliers. Preserve the original sample before any approved evaluation consumes it, and identify any pieces separated for technical review.

Use missing-label containment for the different finished-pack question, splice records for a joint-related break and release-liner selection for material scope.

The artwork is fine. That does not mean the roll is fine.

Retain the backing before a web-break complaint sample is discarded. The attractive face of the label tells only half the story. The liner carries the labels through winding, transport and dispensing; damage hidden underneath can become visible only when the line places it under load.

The buying mistake is to specify paper or PET liner and stop there. Material selection matters, but it does not replace converting quality or a trial on the intended machine.

What kiss cutting is supposed to achieve

For ordinary pressure-sensitive roll labels, the die separates the label shape through the face material and adhesive while preserving a usable continuous release liner. Incomplete separation creates one set of problems; excessive cutting into the backing creates another.

Avery Dennison's converting guidance for a thin PET liner distinguishes a visible die impression from excessive deformation and recommends evaluation across the web and through the run. Its tooling and tension settings are specific to that construction, not universal numbers for every roll.

Two purchasing situations, two sensible thresholds

In a composite juice-brand example, an operator hand-peels labels for a short local launch. A lightly marked backing may never cause a practical interruption. The brand's immediate concern is clean label removal and a good appearance on the bottle.

A co-packer takes the same artwork into an automatic line. Now the liner must stay intact through the complete web path. A defect that was invisible to the founder can cause repeated stops. The co-packer is not being difficult by asking for more evidence; it is buying a different level of process reliability.

Do not reject every visible mark by appearance alone. Agree an inspection method and relevant application evaluation before bulk supply.

Read the location of the break

PatternInvestigation prioritySample to retain
Repeated break at a label cornerDie pattern and local liner damageSeveral complete repeats
Break starts at the web edgeEdge nick, handling or slitting damageBoth edge and surrounding web
Failure at a spliceSplice construction and machine compatibilityThe whole joint
Break changes with machine conditionsWeb path and operating setup with the equipment supplierGood and failed rolls with run notes

These patterns prioritize inspection; they are not a diagnosis on their own. A damaged edge can coexist with an unsuitable machine setting. Changing several variables together makes it harder to know which correction worked.

How to raise a useful complaint

Stop and isolate equipment according to site procedures before retrieving a failed web. Keep the two sides of the break together, mark the direction of travel and record the roll number. Include neighboring labels so the converter can compare the failure to the die repeat.

Photograph the roll edge and core label as well as the close-up. Note whether the issue occurs at the start, middle or end of a roll and whether a previously accepted roll runs on the same setup. Do not cut away the defect just to make a neat sample.

A buyer could ask the converter to inspect retained material from the same lot and review the actual stock-and-tool combination. If the label has changed from paper to film, or the liner thickness has changed, 'same artwork' does not establish that the old tooling remains appropriate.

Write acceptance around performance

  • Identify the complete face stock, adhesive and liner construction.
  • Specify label shape, roll width, winding direction and roll dimensions.
  • Agree samples from relevant lanes and parts of the production run.
  • Define liner inspection and dispensing-trial methods with the suppliers.
  • Document acceptable splices and how affected rolls will be identified.

For material selection, compare glassine and PET release liners. For failures at joins, use the finished-roll splice acceptance guide. A break investigation should preserve evidence before it turns into a debate about who caused the downtime.

Send the relevant project details

Discuss the retained liner-break evidence with roll ID, complete adjacent repeat, travel direction, collection location and photographs of both sides. State what remains unknown.
