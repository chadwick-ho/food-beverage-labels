Metallic Label Detail: Review Positive Lines and Reversed Gaps Separately

Gold-effect offers are compared by price while their intended reflectivity, texture and represented stock differ.

Updated: 30 October 2026 | English B2B packaging purchasing guide.

Editorial purchasing guidance. Packaging examples are illustrative.

The same nominal type size can contain a broad metallic stroke and a very narrow reversed opening. Mark both physical features at finished size before accepting a foil-detail promise or substituting a metallic-ink proposal.

Build an identified physical-effect reference before treating foil and metallic-ink offers as equivalent. The reference needs the intended viewing behavior and stock, not only a photograph or color code.

Give the converter a feature coupon, not only a font name

Identify the smallest metallic stroke, the closest neighbouring features and any reversed text or enclosed opening in the actual artwork. Keep the motif at its final printed size. A nominal point size cannot describe all these widths because different letterforms contain different strokes and internal spaces.

A positive feature is the area intended to receive the metallic decoration. A negative or reversed feature is the space intended to remain clear within or between those areas. Do not transfer an accepted positive-line requirement to a reversed gap without the converter's confirmation. They ask different geometry questions.

Pantec's hot-stamping file guide distinguishes positive lines from negative motifs and makes design limits dependent on the embellishment and material. Its published example values are not this order's tolerances. Obtain the actual converter's supported requirements for the proposed construction instead of importing a number from that page.

Include the difficult parts of the real crest or text, not only an easy demonstration word. A vector inspection can identify geometry, while a production-representative sample contributes different evidence about the offered result. Neither an enlarged screen view nor the diagram here proves transfer quality.

Possible design alternatives include opening a particular gap, strengthening a particular stroke, simplifying one motif or moving supporting copy to ordinary print. Have the designer review the actual changed feature. Making the entire logo larger may be unsuitable when panel space or brand hierarchy is fixed.

Keep appearance and detail approvals connected. A metallic-ink alternative may reproduce a different visual effect even when its small content is acceptable. Conversely, an attractive foil reference does not approve every fine feature in a new design. Request the exact tradeoff, associated preparation charge and sample scope, then retain the accepted coupon with the controlled production artwork.

Keep the reference sample identifiable

Give the physical reference a sample ID and record what it represents: supplied product, stock, finish and any known process. If the process is unknown, describe the desired appearance rather than guessing it from a photograph. A printer can propose a route after the target is understood.

Identify which part of the sample matters. A buyer may want the crest's directional flash without wanting its background, texture or entire decoration system. Sending a beautiful sample without those limits invites suppliers to reproduce different attributes.

Review more than the brightest camera angle

Move the physical samples through the agreed relevant views under a consistent light arrangement. Keep the main target and proposed samples together. A still image can help communicate the setup, but it cannot capture every change of a reflective effect or its tactile character.

Do not describe a digital color match as an approved match to metallic reflection. Likewise, do not call every reflective sample foil or every muted sample ink from appearance alone. The supplier should identify its proposed production route.

Compare the full proposal after matching the target

Once the appearance scope is understood, compare artwork detail, coverage, stock, tooling and quantities. A lower quotation may deliberately offer a different visual direction; keep it as an alternative rather than pretending that it is the same specification at a lower price.

KURZ's sheet-fed cold-transfer explanation distinguishes flat cold-transfer decoration from relief effects possible with hot stamping. That product/process context does not establish which equipment or outcome this supplier can offer for a particular roll-label job.

Keep effect and durability approvals linked but separate

The approved physical appearance should identify the finish and stock represented. Ask separately what handling or exposure evidence is required. A glittering sample shown on a desk has not demonstrated scuff resistance or wet-use performance.

Use finish reading checks, foil/emboss layer registration and color-reference planning to resolve those different approval questions.

Short answer

Compare hot foil, cold transfer and metallic-ink proposals against an identified physical target on the intended stock and finish. The process name alone is not an appearance or color guarantee.

Most metallic-label confusion starts with one line in a brief: "gold logo." The buyer imagines jewelry. The designer imagines a bright screen color. The printer prices the process available on its line. All three can be acting reasonably and still approve different products.

Three processes that should not share one vague name

ProcessVisual characterProduction consideration
Hot foilCrisp reflective accent, sometimes tactileHeat, pressure, tooling and make-ready

Metallic inkSofter shimmer from metallic pigmentInk system, coverage and substrate influence

A typical quote comparison that is not really a comparison

Consider a composite wine-label inquiry. One supplier quotes hot foil, one quotes metallic ink and a third writes only "gold effect." The unit prices are placed in a spreadsheet as if the finish were identical. The lowest quote wins until the sample arrives and the logo looks intentionally muted rather than mirror bright.

The metallic-ink supplier did not necessarily do poor work. Metallic ink may be exactly right for a restrained craft aesthetic. The mistake happened earlier: the buyer compared prices before defining the visual target. A fair quote comparison begins after the process, stock and approved effect match.

Where each option wins honestly

  • Hot foil wins when a small logo, crest or border needs sharp premium emphasis on a compatible stock.
  • Cold foil wins when metallic coverage is larger, detailed or part of a high-throughput label program.
  • Metallic ink wins when the brand wants subtlety, print integration and does not need a mirror finish.
  • Metallized film wins when metallic appearance covers much of the label and artwork can use white ink to control where metal shows.

A large bright accent and a restrained metallic line can serve different brand intentions. Select the physical target before defending a production process.

What to put in a metallic label brief

  • Reference photo or physical sample of the desired reflectivity.
  • Exact areas that are metallic and whether they need texture.
  • Label material, finish and exposure to moisture or abrasion.
  • Quantity per artwork and whether tooling is acceptable.
  • Minimum text and line detail in the metallic layer.
  • Requirement for a production-stock sample before approval.
