Food Label Layout Guide

Food Label Layout Requirements: Ingredients, Allergens, Nutrition and Barcode Space

A food label can look beautiful and still fail in production if the required information, barcode space and package curve are not planned before proofing.

Last updated: September 7, 2026 | Best for: food jars, sauce bottles, honey jars, frozen food packs, pouches, bakery boxes and deli containers.

Short answer

Before printing food labels, reserve artwork space for product identity, net quantity, ingredient list, allergen declaration, nutrition facts panel when required, manufacturer or distributor information, barcode or QR code, date or lot code area, and storage or use directions when applicable.

Important: a label factory can help make the layout print-ready and readable. It should not replace the brand, importer, co-packer or compliance advisor responsible for the final legal wording.

Why this topic deserves its own page

Many small brands start with a front label design, then discover that ingredients, allergens, net weight, barcode and nutrition facts need more room than expected. The result is rushed resizing, tiny text, labels that wrap too far around a jar, or a second back label ordered after the first proof.

The useful workflow is to plan the information architecture first, then design. That is the difference between a pretty mockup and a food label that can move into production.

Front label, back label or wraparound label?

Use a front label when the package only needs brand information and limited supporting text. Use a back label when ingredient, allergen, nutrition, barcode and business information would crowd the front. Use a wraparound label when the package has enough smooth surface and the artwork needs a single continuous panel.

For curved jars and bottles, do not make the wraparound label too wide. If the label crosses into a sharp curve, edge lift and wrinkles become more likely, especially on cold or wet packaging.

Barcode and QR code planning

GS1 US explains that each product variation may need its own barcode. That means flavor, size, package count or bundle changes can affect barcode planning before you print. For packaging artwork, the practical issue is simple: decide barcode placement before the final proof, not after the visual design is finished.

Material choice depends on layout too

If the label carries tiny ingredients, nutrition text or barcode data, material and finish affect readability. Gloss film can look bright but may reflect light. Clear film can look premium but needs enough contrast. Textured paper can feel premium but may not be the best choice for wet or refrigerated handling.

For cold food packs, sauce jars and wet deli containers, review BOPP, PET or laminated options before approving small text and barcode areas.

Quote checklist for print-ready food labels

  • Package type and photos: jar, bottle, pouch, tray, box or container.
  • Label size and available flat area in mm or inches.
  • Front label, back label, lid label, seal strip or wraparound requirement.
  • Artwork file and final information blocks that must appear on the label.
  • Storage condition: dry shelf, refrigerated, frozen, wet handling or oily use.
  • Barcode or QR code needs, including whether scanning must work for retail scanning.

FAQ

Can I print the nutrition facts panel very small?

Do not shrink nutrition or ingredient text just to fit a small front label. Confirm the required format and readability for your market, then choose a larger label, back label or wraparound layout if needed.

Should the barcode go on the front or back label?

Most retail packaging places the barcode on a back or side area where it can scan cleanly without interrupting the front brand panel. Avoid seams, deep curves and low-contrast artwork.

Can one label design work for the U.S. and EU?

Sometimes, but do not assume it. U.S. and EU food information rules can require different wording, language, allergen handling, units and nutrition presentation. Confirm the target market before final artwork.
