Digital or Flexo Labels: Compare the Next Order and a Revised SKU

A grand-total quantity is used to select a printing process while setup reuse and likely SKU changes remain unpriced.

Updated: 21 October 2026 | English B2B packaging buyer guide.

Editorial purchasing guidance. Packaging examples are illustrative.

A first-order quote can favor one process while the next unchanged reorder favors another. A copy revision can change the comparison again. The useful question is how the actual supplier prices those different events for each artwork.

Request a small scenario table instead of a universal digital-to-flexo crossover quantity. Keep the construction and required deliverables equal, then compare first order, unchanged repeat and a named revision scenario.

AI-generated packaging planning example. Willow Kitchen is a fictional brand. No actual customer job, equipment capability, compatibility test or measured result is depicted.

Ask both routes to price the same three events

Use confirmed quantities per artwork and a frozen construction. Mark whether each setup or tooling item is included, separately charged or reusable under stated conditions. The following table is a request structure, not an estimate.

ScenarioDigital routeFlexographic route

Unchanged reorderRepeat charges and any minimum conditionsConfirm which preparation is actually reusable
Named artwork changeIdentify affected files, proof and finishing workIdentify affected separations, tools and preparation

Keep freight, taxes and inventory assumptions on the same basis. Do not add a setup charge again if the finished total already includes it. If a quote is incomplete, resolve the scope before comparing unit prices.

Make uncertainty visible without inventing a forecast

One unchanged repeat and one copy revision are scenarios, not predictions. The business may know that a seasonal SKU ends soon while its best-selling SKU repeats reliably. Use that evidence to choose which cases deserve attention. If probabilities are unknown, show the outcomes separately instead of generating a precise expected-cost number.

Compare total usable demand over the relevant horizon with quantities that must be purchased or held. A lower press price does not resolve unused stock. Conversely, frequent small purchases may involve repeated handling and freight. Record those actual terms rather than assuming either process always wins.

Separate process flexibility from the converter's offered workflow

Digital does not automatically mean every finish or serialized-data service is included. Flexo does not automatically mean a dedicated ink or existing plate can be reused on a different stock. Ask each converter to identify the process, preparation and finishing arrangement represented by its quotation.

HP's official Indigo overview illustrates a broad digital label portfolio; it does not calculate this job's costs or confirm this website's press inventory. Keep equipment capability examples separate from a binding offered scope.

Short answer

Digital printing usually wins when quantities are smaller, artwork changes often or many SKUs share an order. Flexographic printing usually wins when one design repeats at stable volume and setup can be spread over a longer run. Both can produce retail-ready food and beverage labels. The right process is an economic and operational fit, not a quality ranking.

The number of artworks can change the comparison even when the total label count stays the same. Ten thousand labels from one file are not the same production problem as ten thousand labels divided among twenty flavors.

Manufacturer capability descriptions cannot calculate the cost of this specific job. A small label with four colors and one SKU may cross over differently from a large clear label using white ink and eight seasonal designs.

A composite inquiry: the biggest total order is not the longest run

This is a composite planning example, not a claimed customer order. A sauce brand requests 60,000 labels: 30,000 original, 10,000 mild, 8,000 hot, 5,000 garlic and seven test flavors at 1,000 each. The purchasing sheet calls it a 60,000-label job and assumes flexo must be cheaper.

In this hypothetical example, preparation and changeover need to be assessed by SKU. Digital may be worth quoting for the launch mix, while flexo may remain worth quoting for a stable high-volume artwork. No winning cost is established without actual offers.

The lesson is not that digital is better. It is that aggregate volume can hide short runs. Ask for quantity per artwork, not only quantity per purchase order.

Color consistency needs a defined target

Both processes can print consistent color when files, profiles, materials and controls are managed. Problems start when “match the screen” is the only specification. A backlit RGB display, coated paper sample and clear BOPP label do not show color the same way.

  • Identify critical brand colors and whether a measured target exists.
  • State whether the label prints on white, clear, silver or textured stock.
  • Approve a physical proof or production sample for sensitive colors.
  • Keep the approved sample and color notes with future purchase orders.

Flexo can use dedicated spot inks when the job justifies them. Digital presses may reproduce the target through an expanded gamut or a press-specific ink set. Neither method should promise every Pantone color on every substrate without review. Read our Pantone vs CMYK guide before treating a color name as a production result.

Finishing can erase a simple press-price comparison

The printed image is only one stage. Lamination, varnish, die cutting, foil, slitting, inspection and roll packing can create the real bottleneck. A digital print job that requires an uncommon finishing path may not retain its apparent lead-time advantage. A flexo run with in-line coating and die cutting may move efficiently once setup is complete.

When each method honestly wins

Digital is usually the better starting point for pilots, multiple flavors, regional versions, frequent changes and lower quantities per artwork. It also supports variable data when the press and workflow are designed for it.

Flexo is usually the better starting point for stable repeat work, larger quantities per artwork, dedicated spot colors and programs where established tooling and predictable schedules can be reused.

A hybrid purchasing strategy is often stronger than loyalty to one process. Launch digitally, measure which SKUs turn, then evaluate flexo for the proven volume. Keep small seasonal and regional versions digital if that preserves agility.

What to send for a useful comparison quote

  • Finished label dimensions and dieline shape.
  • Quantity for every artwork, not only the grand total.
  • Material, adhesive, laminate or varnish requirements.
  • Number of colors, white ink, spot colors and metallic details.
  • Expected reorder frequency and likely artwork changes.
  • Application method, roll direction, core and maximum roll diameter.
  • One physical reference for any color or finish that is commercially critical.
