Wine Bottle Adhesion: Cross the Bottle Lots with the Label Lots

Bottle and label deliveries change together, but the subsequent appearance difference is attributed to one input without isolating the other.

Updated: 22 October 2026 | English B2B packaging buyer guide.

Editorial purchasing guidance. Packaging examples are illustrative.

The new bottles arrived at the same time as a new label delivery. Comparing an old labeled bottle with a new labeled bottle changes both inputs, so the visible difference cannot be attributed to the glass coating alone.

Where suitable retained materials are available, compare bottle and label lots independently under an agreed plan. A crossed comparison can reveal which relationship needs review without pretending to identify a chemical cause.

Identify what changed before choosing a test

List the bottle item/lot and the exact printed label construction/lot in each observation. Check whether artwork, cut shape, finish or application conditions also changed. A crossed grid is useful only when its inputs can be identified; it does not erase unrecorded differences.

Ask the responsible technical team whether the retained bottles and label rolls are suitable comparison materials. An old roll with unknown storage history should not silently serve as a fresh control. Identify any age or condition limitation before interpreting a result.

Keep the common application conditions explicit

Record the intended fill state, application route and observation intervals. Avoid changing the handling method between cells while claiming that only the bottle or label changed. Where differences cannot be avoided, list them with the observation.

Use the same definition of the observed feature: edge position, visible separation or another agreed outcome. A comparison made immediately in one cell and after storage in another is not a matched observation. Photographs need sample identity, orientation and known observation time.

Read a pattern as a next question

If a difference follows the bottle lot across the represented label lots, that supports further bottle/surface investigation under the sampled conditions. It does not prove that a named coating caused the difference. Ask the bottle supplier to identify relevant finish or treatment information and involve the converter in the review.

If it follows the label lot, investigate the represented construction and its history. If only one combination changes, consider an interaction rather than forcing all outcomes into a single supplier-fault explanation. If the observations are inconsistent or the sample limits dominate, keep the conclusion unresolved.

Record missing cells instead of inventing a control

The retained old label may no longer be available. Mark that cell unavailable and discuss another supported comparison. A current strip described as equivalent is a substitute, not the missing historical object. The same applies to bottle lots identified only from an old photograph.

An official AD Procerta glass comparison illustrates why actual glass surfaces matter. Its reported results belong to its own samples and methods; none supplies a diagnosis or acceptance limit for the hypothetical grid here.

Use embossed-glass orientation, adhesive range coverage and sample onset observations for the separate geometry, selection and timing questions.

The adhesive touches a treated surface

When one wine-label roll sticks to last month's bottles but flags on a new delivery, A buyer could not immediately condemn the roll. Wine glass can carry anti-scuff or slip treatments, and the effective surface can vary by bottle lot. The right comparison is the same printed construction applied to known good and suspect bottles under the same line conditions.

Avery Dennison's glass-substrate comparison reports meaningful differences among bottle glass, other container glass and a laboratory test panel, including effects of texture and coatings. That does not prove any particular coating caused a customer's failure; it shows why a generic glass datasheet is an incomplete diagnosis.

One label, two bottle deliveries

Imagine a composite winery that changes glass supplier while keeping artwork and paper stock. The new bottle looks nearly identical, but a label edge begins to lift after packing. A buyer could separate samples by bottle lot, then note whether lift starts immediately, after a cold room, or after wet service. Timing points to different questions.

A composite contract bottler uses both glossy and satin-finish glass for different brands. It may be tempted to stock one premium label material for all of them. A buyer could test both surfaces first. A heavier textured face and larger label can put more restoring force at the edge, so material simplification has a limit.

Run a controlled comparison

Use bottles from each delivery with the same fill, label roll, application pressure and dwell period. Photograph edges at the same intervals. Record bottle cleanliness and temperature. A face that lifts only after immersion deserves a different conversation from a face that never wets out during initial application.

Send the diagnostic set

  • Good and failed filled bottles, separated by lot.
  • Bottle supplier and any known finish or coating change.
  • Exact printed label roll, dieline and material construction.
  • Application temperature, moisture and wipe-down settings.
  • Photos of edges immediately and after storage or wet service.
